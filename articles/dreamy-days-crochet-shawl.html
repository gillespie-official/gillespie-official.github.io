Some crochet projects are as enjoyable to plan as they are to wear. The Dreamy Days shawl brings together warm brown, creamy neutrals, coral, and shades of pink in a generous triangular shape. Bands of openwork and texture keep the stripes interesting, while a pale scalloped edge and corner tassels finish the piece with a soft, relaxed feel.

Designed by Wilma Westenberg of Wilmade, this shawl has an original pattern and video tutorial available through the designer’s website. This article is a companion for choosing colors, thinking about drape, preparing your supplies, and styling the finished shawl. A Closer Look at the Sunset Color Story

The color sequence is one of the shawl’s strongest features. A warm brown section near the top gives way to cream, peachy coral, lighter pink, and brighter pink bands. The pale outer border provides a quiet frame around those warm colors.

Because the stripes follow the triangular shape, they form a repeated V. That arrangement draws attention toward the point and makes the color transitions part of the structure. A shawl with the same shades arranged differently could feel very different, even before changing any stitches.

The mixture of open spaces and more substantial rows also changes how each stripe looks. A pale color in openwork can seem airy and delicate, while a denser band gives a stronger line. Consider both stitch texture and color when imagining your own version.

Begin With the Designer’s Instructions

Wilmade describes Dreamy Days as a top-down triangle shawl with a four-row repeat. The pattern uses chains, double crochet, and front and back post stitches. The designer’s page provides the specific materials and links to the supporting tutorial; written-pattern access may require a free email login.

Read the notes before starting, especially the conventions for turning chains, the center space, and where the last stitch belongs. A familiar stitch name does not guarantee that every pattern handles its edges the same way.

Keep the instructions somewhere easy to consult while working. Mark your place in your own notes instead of relying on memory after a break. A repeating pattern can become relaxing, but only once you understand how the rows connect.

Check the Skills You Want to Practice

If post stitches are new to you, practice them on a small sample first. Notice where the hook travels in relation to the existing stitch and how the resulting texture changes between the front and back. A small practice piece gives you room to learn without tracking the whole shawl.

Also rehearse identifying the final stitch at the end of a row. Missed edge stitches can change the outline of a triangle gradually, which makes them easy to overlook at first. Good habits at the beginning are worth more than a hurried start.

Choose Yarn for a Shawl You Want to Wear

A shawl sits close to the neck and shoulders, so comfort matters. Think about the texture you enjoy against your skin, the seasons when you expect to wear it, and the care routine you can realistically follow. A beautiful yarn is most useful when it suits your actual wardrobe.

If you substitute for the suggested yarn, compare more than the weight label. Fiber, construction, density, and drape all influence the result. Two yarns that look similar in a ball may behave differently once a wide fabric hangs from your shoulders.

Follow the pattern’s gauge and materials guidance, then make a sample using the stitch pattern where practical. Handle the sample, fold it, and let it hang over your hand. You want to understand how the fabric moves before investing in a full shawl.

Wash the Sample Before Committing

Treat the sample according to the yarn’s care instructions and let it dry completely. Compare its size and feel with your earlier observations. Some fabrics relax after washing, and that change matters in a large accessory.

Write down the hook used and the measurements before and after care. If you test another hook, label the second sample immediately. It is surprisingly easy to forget which piece produced the fabric you preferred.

Plan Your Own Stripe Palette

You can borrow the warm-to-bright progression without copying every shade. Begin with a grounding color, add a lighter transition, and choose one or two more expressive colors toward the outer part of the triangle. Finish with a border that gives the entire group a clear edge.

Lay the yarns in the order you expect to use them. Look for transitions that feel intentional. If two neighboring colors nearly disappear into each other, that may create a gentle blend; if you want distinct stripes, choose a stronger difference.

Remember that later rows of a growing triangle cover more area than early rows. A color used near the outer edge can have much more visual presence than one used near the beginning. It may also require more yarn for the same number of rows.

Make a Simple Stripe Map

Write each color name on a separate line in your project notebook and leave space for the rows it will cover. Follow the designer’s color-change schedule if you want the pictured balance. If you create a variation, record the actual changes as you work so the plan and the finished project do not drift apart.

Drape Is Part of the Design

A shawl is a moving piece of fabric. A sample that looks beautiful lying flat may feel too stiff around your shoulders. Judge the washed sample by how it hangs as well as how closely its measurements match the pattern.

Keep the Triangle Growing Evenly

A triangle shawl depends on consistent shaping at its designated increase points. Keep those locations easy to recognize and follow the pattern’s exact treatment of them. The center should remain a reliable landmark, not a place where you improvise when the count feels uncertain.

Use a marker if it helps identify the center space, and move it as the instructions require. Check the two sides regularly. They should relate to each other according to the pattern rather than slowly developing different lengths or stitch counts.

Lay the shawl on a broad surface every few repeats. When you see the whole piece, you can notice a drifting center, a tight edge, or a misplaced stripe earlier. The fabric may not look fully settled until finishing, but clear asymmetry deserves investigation.

Make Long Rows Feel Manageable

The opening rows can move quickly, while later rows take more time. Plan for that change instead of judging every session by the number of rows completed. Finishing one long, accurate row is meaningful progress.

Break the work at a point you can identify easily when you return. Record the current repeat and whether you have completed the row. Keep the yarn, hook, and notes together so a short crafting window does not disappear into searching for supplies.

Let your hands and shoulders relax periodically. A large shawl can become heavy in your lap, and supporting it comfortably helps you maintain steadier tension. Arrange the fabric so its weight is not pulling constantly on the active stitches.

Pro Tip: Set aside the border and tassel yarn before working the longest stripes. Finishing details deserve their place in the yarn budget from the start.

Give the Border Room in Your Yarn Plan

The pale scalloped border is an important part of the shawl’s character. It softens the geometric stripes and gives the outer edge a finished rhythm. Treat it as part of the original yarn plan, rather than hoping leftover scraps will be enough.

Before beginning the edging, confirm that you have reached the stage required by the pattern. Decorative borders often depend on specific stitch counts or repeat placement. Starting from the wrong foundation can make an otherwise correct border bunch or stretch.

As you work, check that the edging sits comfortably against the shawl. A pulled edge or overly loose section may need a tension adjustment or a review of placement. Follow the tutorial where the border turns corners or reaches the point.

Finish Tassels With Consistent Proportions

The light tassels at the corners echo the border color and add movement. Make them according to the designer’s instructions, then compare their lengths before attaching them. Small differences become more visible when the shawl is worn symmetrically.

Secure the tassels carefully and check how their weight affects the corners. They should feel like a finishing detail, not an awkward pull. Keep a little matching yarn for later maintenance in case the ties need attention after use.

Block and Finish According to the Yarn

Finishing helps the texture open and the outline settle. Use a method appropriate to the fiber and the pattern’s care guidance. Do not assume that aggressive stretching or high heat will improve every shawl.

Spread the piece on a suitable surface and shape it evenly. Pay attention to both sides of the triangle and to the rhythm of the scallops. Aim for a balanced outline rather than pulling individual points to an extreme.

Let the shawl dry fully before judging its final drape or trimming any details that depend on its settled length. Weave in ends securely and inspect color changes. A little extra time here makes the shawl more pleasant to wear and easier to care for.

Style a Colorful Triangle Shawl Simply

The photographed shawl works beautifully with a plain light top and jeans because the clothing leaves room for the stitches and stripes. You can use the same principle with a simple dress, a neutral cardigan, or a solid-color blouse.

Wear the triangle across the back for a classic wrap, or bring the point forward for a scarf-like arrangement when the size and drape allow it. Adjust gently rather than pulling hard on the tassels or border.

If your wardrobe already contains strong patterns, choose a shawl palette that repeats one or two familiar colors. A handmade accessory can be expressive and still feel easy to wear. The goal is to make something you reach for, not something that remains folded away.

Keep Useful Notes for the Next Shawl

Record the colors, hook, yarn quantities used, and any departures from the original plan. Take a photograph of the finished shawl spread flat as well as one showing its drape. Those views answer different questions when you look back.

Note which part of the project needed the most concentration. Perhaps the center shaping became comfortable quickly, while the border needed a quieter session. Knowing that helps you plan the next project around your own making habits.

Save the original designer’s link with those notes so you can return to updates or supporting tutorials. A good project record combines your experience with the pattern’s reliable instructions, giving you a stronger starting point for future color experiments.

A Little Inspiration to Take With You

A sunset-striped crochet shawl is a chance to enjoy color slowly, one growing row at a time. Start with the original Dreamy Days instructions, choose a palette that belongs in your wardrobe, and give the drape and finishing as much attention as the stitches. The reward is a thoughtful handmade layer with warmth, movement, and a little everyday joy.
